# Understanding Childhood Cancer: Signs and Symptoms to Watch For

A fever that keeps coming back. Bruises you can’t explain. A child who suddenly seems tired all the time.

Most of the time, these symptoms have ordinary explanations. But childhood cancer can begin with signs that look surprisingly similar to common illnesses or everyday childhood complaints. The challenge for parents and caregivers is not to diagnose cancer themselves. It is knowing when an ordinary symptom has become a pattern worth checking.

Every year, an estimated [400,000 children and adolescents around the world are diagnosed with cancer](https://peopledaily.digital/news/childhood-cancer-day-who-highlights-common-types-of-cancer-in-children). In high-income countries, over 80% of these children survive. But in low- and middle-income communities, survival rates can fall below 30%. Some estimates place the global number even higher, [the International Agency for Research on Cancer found that almost 280,000 children and adolescents were diagnosed in 2020 alone](https://www.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/pr308_E.pdf), and the true number may be higher still, since childhood cancer is difficult to diagnose in many parts of the world.

The difference is not simply the cancer itself. Delayed or missed diagnoses, difficulty accessing care, unavailable treatment, and other barriers such as the inability of the family to pay for the treatment all contribute to this survival gap.

Childhood cancer can be especially difficult to recognize because its early signs can look like things children experience all the time: fever, tiredness, headaches, bruises, aches, or swollen lymph nodes.

**So what should you actually look for?**

Not one isolated symptom, but a change that persists, gets worse, keeps coming back, seems unusual for your child, or appears alongside other unexplained symptoms.

This guide will help you understand those patterns, know what deserves a closer look, and recognize when it is time to talk to a healthcare professional.

## Why Childhood Cancer Is So Easy to Miss

Unlike many adult cancers, childhood cancers generally cannot be prevented through lifestyle changes or detected through routine population screening. Their causes are often not fully understood. And because children can't always describe how they're feeling, it often falls to parents, teachers, and caregivers to notice that "something isn't quite right."

[The most common cancers in children](https://www.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/pr308_E.pdf) include leukaemia, brain and central nervous system tumours, lymphomas, and solid tumours like neuroblastoma and Wilms tumour. Each one can show up differently, which is why it helps to know the broader patterns to watch for, rather than just one or two symptoms.

Instead of memorizing a long checklist, it can be more helpful to ask three questions:

- Is this symptom unexplained?
- Is it persisting or getting worse?
- Is it appearing with other unusual changes?

## Common Signs and Symptoms to Watch For

Keep in mind, having one of these symptoms does **not** mean your child has cancer. Most of the time, it won't be. But if a symptom is unexplained, persistent, or getting worse instead of better, it's always worth a conversation with your child's doctor. [WHO points to symptoms lasting more than two weeks, or progressively worsening, as a key warning flag](https://peopledaily.digital/news/childhood-cancer-day-who-highlights-common-types-of-cancer-in-children).

- **Unexplained, ongoing fever** that doesn't go away with normal treatment or has no obvious cause like a cold or flu
- **Unusual tiredness or weakness** that doesn't improve with rest, or a child who seems unusually pale — [described clinically as "protracted pallor and malaise"](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2013/0801/p185)
- **Unexplained weight loss** or a sudden loss of appetite
- **A lump or swelling**, especially one that is painless, in the neck, abdomen, armpit, groin, or limbs — [persistently enlarged lymph nodes are a recognized red flag](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2013/0801/p185)
- **Persistent bone or joint pain**, especially pain that wakes a child up at night, or unexplained limping — [ongoing bone pain that disturbs sleep is flagged as a warning sign for bone tumours](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2013/0801/p185)
- **Easy bruising or bleeding**, frequent nosebleeds, or tiny red spots on the skin
- **Frequent headaches**, especially ones that happen in the morning and come with vomiting — [morning headaches with vomiting can point to central nervous system tumours](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2013/0801/p185)
- **Sudden changes in vision**, a new squint, or a white glow in a child's eye in photographs — [a classic sign of retinoblastoma known as leukokoria](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2013/0801/p185)
- **Ongoing swollen lymph nodes** that don't shrink after a few weeks
- **Changes in balance, walking, or speech**, or a loss of skills a child had already learned

**The key is not one symptom on its own, but a change that persists, worsens, or simply doesn’t seem right for your child.** You know what is normal for your child. If something feels unusual, it is worth getting checked.

## What to Do If You're Worried

If a symptom concerns you, talk to your family doctor or pediatrician. [Timely diagnosis and treatment are key to improving a child's chances of survival](https://together.stjude.org/en-us/blog/article/early-diagnosis-of-childhood-cancer-saves-lives.html). Most symptoms will not turn out to be cancer. But when something is persistent, unexplained, or getting worse, seeking medical advice can help your child get the right diagnosis and care sooner.

If your child is diagnosed with cancer, you are not alone. Families around the world face the same uncertainty, but not every family has equal access to the tests, treatment, and support their child needs.
