# Liver Cancer Screening and Treatment: Understanding Your Options

*Written by Nusaiba Mohammed*

Liver cancer can be difficult to detect early because symptoms may not appear until the disease has progressed. For people at higher risk, regular screening can help detect cancer earlier, when more treatment options may be available.

Screening means looking for cancer before a person develops symptoms. However, liver cancer screening is not routinely recommended for everyone.

People at higher risk, including some individuals living with chronic hepatitis B or cirrhosis, may need regular monitoring. This can include an ultrasound of the liver, sometimes combined with a blood test for a protein called [alpha-fetoprotein (AFP)](https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diagnostics/24301-alpha-fetoprotein-test). The recommended approach depends on a person's medical history and level of risk.

For people who are not already being monitored, liver cancer may be investigated when symptoms or routine test results raise concerns. This is why it's helpful to understand your personal risk and ask your healthcare provider whether monitoring is appropriate for you.

## What Happens If a Test Shows Something Unusual?

Depending on the situation, doctors may recommend blood tests, an ultrasound, a CT scan, or an MRI. Sometimes a biopsy is needed, but liver cancer can often be diagnosed through imaging without taking a tissue sample.

Waiting for results can be difficult. People may find themselves imagining the worst, searching online for answers, or struggling to concentrate on everyday responsibilities. Others may keep their worries to themselves because they don't want to upset their families.

There is no single way to react to uncertainty. If you're going through testing, it may be helpful to write down your questions before appointments, bring someone you trust along, or ask your healthcare team to explain anything that isn't clear. You don't have to understand everything in one conversation.

## What Treatment Options Are Available?

Treatment depends on several factors, including the stage of the cancer, where it is located, how well the liver is functioning, and the person's overall health. Two people with liver cancer may receive different types of treatments because their medical situations are different.

Some treatment options include:

- **Surgery:** Removing the part of the liver containing the cancer when this is suitable.
- **Liver transplant:** Replacing the liver in selected cases when a person meets the medical criteria.
- **Ablation:** Using heat or other methods to destroy cancer cells in a specific area.
- **Embolization treatments:** Directing treatment to the blood vessels supplying the tumour, sometimes alongside medication.
- **Targeted therapy and immunotherapy:** Medicines that target particular features of cancer cells or help the immune system fight cancer.
- **Radiation therapy:** Using radiation to damage cancer cells in selected situations.

Some people may receive a combination of the treatment options. The healthcare team can explain the potential benefits, side effects, and goals of each approach.

## Asking for Help Is Part of the Process

It is important to remember that people cope differently. One person may want to discuss every detail, while another may need time before talking about their feelings. Asking what kind of support someone wants, rather than assuming what they need, can make a real difference.

A social worker, counsellor, or member of the healthcare team may be able to help with emotional concerns, family conversations, practical arrangements, and connections to community resources. Some cancer centres and organizations also offer support groups where people can speak with others who understand what they are experiencing.

If you're supporting a family member with liver cancer, remember that your well-being matters too. You can care deeply about someone and still feel tired, worried, or unsure of what to do. Taking time to rest, asking others to share responsibilities, and talking to someone you trust can help you manage the strain.

## A Final Thought

Learning about liver cancer screening and treatment can make an unfamiliar process easier to understand. If you're at increased risk, ask your healthcare provider whether regular monitoring is right for you. If you've received an unusual test result or a diagnosis, ask questions until you understand the next steps and the options available.

And remember that cancer care involves more than tests and treatment plans. Emotional well-being, family relationships, finances, and everyday responsibilities all matter too.

Sometimes, the most helpful thing we can do for someone facing cancer is to listen without rushing to offer answers, ask what they need, and help them find the right support.

You can also play a part by sharing this blog with friends and family or supporting ACCE's **No Patient Left Behind initiative**. Helping patients access treatment and the practical support they need can make a difficult time a little easier to navigate.
