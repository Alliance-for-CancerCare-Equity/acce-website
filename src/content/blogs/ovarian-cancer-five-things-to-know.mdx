# Ovarian Cancer: 5 Things Every Woman Should Know

Ovarian cancer can be difficult to detect in its early stages. Its symptoms can be subtle, and signs like [bloating, pelvic discomfort or changes in appetite](https://ovariancanada.org/symptoms-detection) can easily be mistaken for everyday health concerns.

But knowing what is normal for your body and recognizing when something changes can make a difference.

In 2026, an estimated 3,000 women in Canada will be diagnosed with ovarian cancer, and approximately 1,950 will die from the disease.

Here are five things every woman should know about ovarian cancer.

## 1. Ovarian cancer is not just one disease

Ovarian cancer begins when abnormal cells grow uncontrollably in or around the ovaries and can spread to nearby tissues. The ovaries are part of the female reproductive system and are located on each side of the uterus. They produce eggs (ova) and hormones such as estrogen and progesterone.

There are different types of ovarian cancer, and each type can develop differently. Some can develop and spread before noticeable symptoms appear, which is one reason ovarian cancer can be difficult to detect early.

## 2. Your family history matters

While people with ovaries have some risk for ovarian cancer, some have higher risk than others based on a number of factors including:

**Age**: The risk increases with age, with ovarian cancer occurring more commonly among people between ages 50 and 79.

**Family history:** A family history of ovarian, breast, prostate, pancreatic, endometrial or colorectal cancer may indicate an inherited cancer risk.

**Inherited genetic mutations:** Changes in genes such as **BRCA1 and BRCA2** can increase ovarian cancer risk. These mutations are more common in some populations, including people of Ashkenazi Jewish descent, French-Canadian descent, or Icelandic/Dutch descent have an increased risk for ovarian cancer.

**Other gene mutations:** Some cases of ovarian cancer are linked to inherited gene mutations associated with [Lynch syndrome](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/lynch-syndrome/symptoms-causes/syc-20374714), also known as hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer (HNPCC).

If ovarian or breast cancer runs in your family, consider discussing your family history with your healthcare provider. They may recommend genetic counselling to help you better understand your risk and whether genetic testing is appropriate.

## 3. Symptoms can be easy to miss

Ovarian cancer can be difficult to detect early because symptoms may not appear right away. In some cases, the cancer can begin to spread before there are any noticeable signs. This is one reason ovarian cancer is often diagnosed at a later stage, when it can be harder to treat. As a result, ovarian cancer is often diagnosed in (Stages 3 and 4), making survival difficult.

When ovarian cancer first develops, it may not cause any noticeable symptoms. When symptoms do appear, they can easily be mistaken for other, more common health conditions. Here are some signs to look out for:

- Abdominal bloating or swelling
- Quickly feeling full when eating
- Weight loss
- Discomfort in the pelvic area
- Fatigue
- Back pain
- Changes in bowel habits, such as constipation
- A frequent need to urinate

## 4. Screening is still a challenge

There is currently no reliable routine screening test or single test that can diagnose ovarian cancer on its own. You know your body best, so if any of the symptoms above are unusual for you and persist for more than three weeks, talk to your healthcare provider. Depending on your symptoms and risk factors, they may recommend tests such as:

**Transvaginal ultrasound:** This test uses a small ultrasound probe inserted into the vagina to create images of the ovaries and look for any abnormalities.

**Pelvic exam:** A healthcare provider examines the abdomen and pelvic area to check for any unusual changes, such as swelling or masses.

**CA125 blood test:** This test measures the level of a protein called cancer antigen 125 (CA125) in the blood. CA125 is not a reliable test for detecting ovarian cancer early because levels can also rise for reasons unrelated to cancer, including menstruation.

## 5. Treatment options are available

Treatment for ovarian cancer depends on the type and stage of the cancer and may include surgery, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, immunotherapy or other treatments.

Researchers are also continuing to study new ways to treat ovarian cancer. If you are interested in exploring additional options, ask your care team whether a clinical trial may be right for you.

## Know the signs. Speak up. Spread the Word.

Ovarian cancer awareness is not about living in fear. It is about having the right information to recognize changes, understand your personal risk and seek medical advice when something does not feel right.

If you or someone you know is experiencing persistent or concerning symptoms, encourage them to speak with a healthcare professional. If they are concerned about the cost of treatment, organizations like the Alliance for CancerCare Equity (ACCE) are continuing to support cancer patients who cannot afford treatment.

You can also play a part by sharing this blog with friends and family or by supporting ACCE's **No Patient Left Behind initiative**. Every action helps bring hope, support, and access to care for patients who need it most.
