# Rare Cancer: 5 Things Patients and Families Should Know

*Written by Elaine Ho*

**When cancer is rare, finding answers can sometimes be just as difficult as hearing the diagnosis.**

“You have cancer” is difficult enough to hear.

But what happens when the next sentence is: “It’s rare”?

You may suddenly have questions you never expected to ask. *What exactly do I have? Has my doctor treated this before? What are my options? Where do I find someone who understands this cancer?*

You do not need to have all the answers today. But knowing what to ask and where to look can help you take the next step.

Here are five things to know.

## 1. Know the Exact Name of Your Cancer

With a rare cancer, the details matter. The specific type and subtype can affect your treatment options and the information that is most relevant to you.

Ask your doctor to write down the full name and subtype of your cancer, and keep a copy of your pathology report and important test results. Having these details on hand can make it easier to ask questions, seek a second opinion, or find reliable information about your diagnosis.

## 2. Find a Doctor Who Knows Your Cancer

Your doctor may be an experienced cancer specialist but have limited experience with your particular rare cancer. It is okay to ask, **“How often have you treated this type of cancer?”**

If your cancer is unfamiliar to your healthcare team, ask whether you can see a specialist or visit a cancer centre with more experience with that cancer type. A second opinion may also help confirm your diagnosis, offer another perspective on treatment, or give you more confidence about what comes next.

## 3. Ask: “What Are All My Options?”

When a treatment is recommended, take time to understand why it may be right for you. Ask about other options, possible benefits and side effects, and whether there are clinical trials you may be eligible for. For some rare cancers, clinical trials may be worth discussing, especially when treatment options are limited.

Write down your questions before each appointment and consider bringing someone you trust to listen or take notes. Having support can make it easier to remember important information and make decisions about your care.

## 4. Find Your Rare Cancer Community

Having a rare cancer can feel isolating, especially when you do not know anyone who has heard of your diagnosis. But rare cancer organizations and patient communities can connect you with people who have faced similar questions and experiences.

Ask your healthcare team about organizations or support groups for your specific cancer. Your community does not have to be nearby. The right connection may be in another city or even another country, and may help you find reliable information, specialists, support services, and other resources.

If you’re in Canada, for example, you can explore organizations such as the [Canadian Organization for Rare Disorders (CORD)](https://www.raredisorders.ca/) or the [Canadian Rare Disease Network (CRDN)](https://canadianrdn.ca/) as a starting point. In Ghana, the [Rare Disease Ghana Initiative (RDGI)](https://rarediseaseghana.org/) is another resource for people and families affected by rare diseases.

## 5. Ask About Support Before You Need It

The cost of cancer can go beyond treatment. Seeing a specialist may mean travelling hours from home, and transportation, accommodation, meals, childcare, parking, and time away from work can quickly add up.

Ask about support early, before these costs become overwhelming. A social worker, patient navigator, hospital, cancer organization, or community program may be able to connect you with financial or practical support. A simple question like, **“Is there any financial or practical support available to me?”** can be a good place to start.

## You Don’t Need Every Answer Today

With a rare cancer, there may not always be a clear roadmap. You do not need to figure it all out at once. Start with what you can do today.

**Know your diagnosis. Ask questions. Find the right expertise. Explore your options. Reach out for support.**

That last step matters to us at the [Alliance for CancerCare Equity (ACCE)](/who-we-are). We provide financial assistance to patients in Canada and Ghana, while our patient navigator in Ghana helps people understand their options and find the resources they need.

Rare should never mean navigating cancer alone.
